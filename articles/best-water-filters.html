The short answer is reverse osmosis. It is the only household technology that removes PFAS, microplastics, lead and fluoride together, because it separates by membrane exclusion rather than adsorption. A certified carbon block covers lead, PFAS and microplastics but leaves fluoride and nitrates untouched. Standard pitcher and refrigerator filters cover chlorine taste and very little else. Which of those you actually need depends on your own water, so this guide starts there.

Every water filter roundup has the same structural flaw. It ranks products against an imaginary average tap, then hands you a winner. But water is the most local thing you consume. Lead does not come from the treatment plant, it comes from the service line under your own front garden. Nitrates track the farmland upstream of you. Chromium 6 tracks local geology and local industry. Two neighbours on different pipe runs can have measurably different water.

So this guide runs in the other order. First find out what is in your supply, which takes about ten minutes and costs nothing. Then match a technology to it. The product list comes last, because by the time you reach it you will already know which section applies to you.

1. Start With Your Water, Not With a Product

There are three sources of truth, and they answer different questions.

1

Your utility's annual report

The Consumer Confidence Report is free, and every community water system is legally required to publish one by July each year. Search your utility's name plus "consumer confidence report". It tells you what was in the water when it left the plant.

2

The EWG Tap Water Database

Enter your zip code at ewg.org/tapwater. It reformats the same utility data against health based guidelines rather than legal limits, so it surfaces contaminants your utility can legally report as fully compliant.

3

A certified home test

The only option that captures what your own pipes and fixtures add after the water leaves the main. This is where lead shows up. Use a lab that is certified by your state, and run a first draw sample after the tap has sat unused overnight.

The gap between the second and third sources is the one people miss. A Consumer Confidence Report describes water at the plant and at sampling points across the network. It cannot describe your house. If your home was built before 1986, or you have brass fixtures of unknown age, or your city's lead service line inventory lists your address as "unknown material", then the only number that means anything is the one from your own tap.

On a private well, none of this applies. Roughly 43 million people in the US drink from private wells, and the EPA does not regulate them at all. No annual report exists, no action level applies, and nobody is testing on your behalf. Well water also skews toward a different contaminant profile: nitrates from agricultural runoff, arsenic and uranium from geology, and bacteria after heavy rain. Annual testing is not optional here, it is the entire safety system.

2. What Changed in 2026

Three regulatory facts are worth knowing before you spend money, because together they explain why the filter is the part of this you actually control.

The PFAS deadline moved. In May 2026 the EPA proposed keeping the federal limits of 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS, while allowing water systems to request two additional years to comply, pushing the deadline from April 2029 to April 2031. The same proposal would rescind the drinking water limits for four other PFAS entirely: PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA (GenX) and the Hazard Index for mixtures. Several states keep their own standards, but at federal level those four compounds would have no limit at all.

Lead pipe replacement runs into the 2030s. The Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, finalized in late 2024, require utilities to replace lead and galvanized service lines at a minimum of 10 percent per year, with full replacement due by 2037 and initial compliance in November 2027. Lead pipes still connect up to 9 million US homes, and the EPA's own inventory lists more than 24 million additional service lines as "unknown material".

Wells were never covered. As above, the 43 million people on private supplies sit outside all of it.

None of this is a reason to panic about tap water, which in most of the US is genuinely well treated for the things it is regulated for. It is a reason to understand that "compliant" and "clean" are different words, and that the timeline for the difference closing is measured in years.

4. The Six Filter Types, and What Each One Really Does

Standard pitcher and faucet filters. Granular activated carbon, pore sizes in the tens of microns. Built to make chlorinated water taste better, and very good at that. Not particle filters, not PFAS filters, and no help with fluoride or nitrates. Cheapest to buy, most expensive per gallon over time.

Certified carbon block pitchers. A denser, compressed carbon medium with pore sizes down to 0.5 microns, which is a genuinely different product from the one above despite looking identical on a shelf. The good ones carry NSF/ANSI 53 for lead, 401 for microplastics and P473 for PFOA and PFOS. Slower flow is the tradeoff, and it is the honest signal that filtration is happening.

Countertop reverse osmosis. A full multi stage RO system that plugs into a wall socket and sits next to the kettle. No plumbing, no drilling, moves house with you. You fill a tank by hand and the filtered water collects in a carafe. The best fit for renters and the most common recommendation we make.

Under sink reverse osmosis. The same membrane, plumbed in, with a dedicated faucet. Tank versions store filtered water in a pressurized bladder, which is convenient but means the water waits in a polymer bladder before you draw it. Tankless versions filter on demand and skip that step. Highest capacity, lowest cost per gallon, needs cabinet space and an installer or a confident afternoon.

Gravity countertop systems. Stainless canisters with ceramic or carbon elements, no power and no plumbing. Popular for off grid and emergency use. The category's problem is that certification is patchy and the strongest marketing claims often sit on the least certified products, which is exactly the situation Berkey is currently in.

Whole house systems. Installed at the point where water enters the building, so every tap, shower and appliance is covered. Excellent for chlorine, sediment and iron. Usually not the right tool for PFAS, because the contact time needed to strip parts per trillion is hard to achieve at whole house flow rates. The standard sensible setup is whole house for volume plus a certified system at the kitchen sink for drinking.

5. How We Chose

Every product below cleared the same five checks we run before anything gets recommended:

  3. Independent certification. WQA or IAPMO certification against the relevant NSF/ANSI standards, or published third party lab results where certification is absent.
  4. Lawsuits. We checked for class actions, consumer protection actions and regulatory actions against every brand.
  5. Recalls. We checked for open recalls on every model listed.

Among the systems that passed all five, owner reviews broke the tie. Certification tells you a unit performed in a lab. Several thousand owners tell you whether it still works in a kitchen after a year.

8. Beyond the Kitchen Tap: Shower, Fridge, Whole House

Shower filters

A ten minute hot shower is a real exposure route, and not the one people assume. You absorb some chlorine through skin, but the larger share arrives as inhaled disinfection byproducts, because hot water volatilizes them into a small enclosed room. If your water smells of chlorine at the tap, your bathroom is concentrating it.

Shower filters are also the one category where expectations should stay low. There is no certified option for chloramine, which is what many US utilities now use instead of free chlorine, and chloramine needs KDF or vitamin C media rather than plain carbon. Nothing at this price point handles PFAS.

Refrigerator filters

Keep it, replace it on schedule, and do not count it as your water filter. Most are certified to NSF/ANSI 42 for chlorine and some to 53 for lead, which is a real if narrow benefit. Pore sizes in the 10 to 20 micron range mean no microplastic reduction, and PFAS certification is close to nonexistent in the category. Worth it in three situations: chlorine or chloramine heavy water that irritates skin and hair throughout the house, well water with sediment or iron that damages fixtures and appliances, or a documented lead service line where you want every tap covered rather than one.

Even with a whole house system, keep a certified filter at the kitchen sink. Whole house handles volume, point of use handles the water you actually swallow.

9. What Does Not Work

Standard pitcher and faucet filters. Granular carbon built for chlorine taste. Pore sizes in the tens of microns, no meaningful PFAS reduction, no fluoride, no nitrates. A genuine taste upgrade, not a contaminant filter, and the filtered water sits in plastic.

Refrigerator filters. As above, with the added problem that people rarely change them on schedule.

Bottled water. A 2024 Columbia University study using laser microscopy counted roughly 240,000 plastic particles per liter in popular brands, around 90 percent of them nanoplastics, which is far more than typical treated tap water. PET bottles shed faster under heat and time, so a case in a warm garage is worse again. Full detail in microplastics in bottled water.

Boiling, mostly. A 2024 study found that boiling hard water forms limescale that traps plastic particles, allowing removal up to around 90 percent when the water is calcium rich. In soft water the effect largely disappears. Boiling does nothing for PFAS, lead, nitrates or fluoride, and because it reduces water volume it slightly concentrates all four.

Alkaline drops, structured water devices and mineral sticks. No particle filtration and no contaminant removal.

Water softeners, as a health filter. Ion exchange softeners swap calcium and magnesium for sodium. Good for scale and appliances, irrelevant for every contaminant in section 3 except hardness.

11. Reading the Certifications

Filter marketing leans hard on numbers that sound official and are not. These are the ones that mean something:

  • NSF/ANSI 42. Aesthetic effects: chlorine, taste and odor. On its own this tells you nothing about health contaminants.
  • NSF/ANSI 53. Health effects, including lead, cysts, VOCs and, on some filters, PFAS.
  • NSF/ANSI 58. Reverse osmosis system performance, including arsenic V.
  • NSF/ANSI 401. Incidental contaminants, and the standard under which microplastic reduction in the 0.5 to 1 micron range is verified. Look for this on any microplastics claim. It does not cover nanoplastics.
  • NSF P473. PFOA and PFOS reduction specifically. The one to look for on PFAS claims from carbon filters.
  • NSF P231. Microbiological purification: bacteria, viruses, cysts. Relevant for wells and travel.
  • NSF/ANSI 177. Shower filter chlorine reduction. The only certification that exists for the shower category.
  • NSF/ANSI 372. Lead free contact materials. A materials standard, not a performance one. A filter certified only to 372 has been checked for what it is made of, not for what it removes.

Certification is issued by different bodies. WQA and IAPMO both certify against NSF/ANSI standards, and either is legitimate. What is not legitimate is the phrase "tested to NSF standards", which appears on a great many product pages and means the manufacturer ran a test itself. Certified and tested to are different claims, and the gap between them is where most of this category's marketing lives.

12. When to Change Your Filter

An expired filter is worse than no filter, because accumulated contaminants can begin releasing back into the water. Set a calendar reminder rather than waiting for a taste change, which is a lagging indicator at best and absent entirely for lead and PFAS.

13. Frequently Asked Questions

What water filter removes PFAS, microplastics, lead and fluoride?

Reverse osmosis removes all four, and it is the only widely available household technology that does. Its membrane rejects PFAS, microplastics and nanoplastics, lead, fluoride, nitrates, arsenic and chromium 6 in one step. A carbon block certified to NSF/ANSI 53 and 401 covers lead, PFAS and microplastics but not fluoride or nitrates, because activated carbon does not adsorb either. Our top pick is the Bluevua RO100ROPOT countertop system, which needs no plumbing and collects filtered water in a borosilicate glass carafe. If you want the most complete third party certification instead, the AquaTru Glass Carafe is certified by IAPMO to NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 58, 401 and 372. If you only need chlorine, lead and taste handled, a certified carbon block filter costs far less and is enough.

How do I find out what is in my tap water?

Three sources, in order. First, your utility's annual Consumer Confidence Report, which is free and legally required to be published every July. Second, the EWG Tap Water Database, which reformats the same utility data against health based guidelines rather than legal limits, so it flags contaminants your utility can legally report as compliant. Third, a certified home test kit, which is the only option that captures what your own pipes and fixtures add after the water leaves the main. If you are on a private well, no utility report exists and testing is the only option.

Do water filters remove PFAS?

Some do, most do not. Reverse osmosis rejects PFAS at the membrane and is the most reliable option. Certain carbon block filters adsorb them well enough to earn NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF P473 certification for PFOA and PFOS, and anion exchange resin also works. What does not work: standard granular carbon pitchers, faucet attachments, refrigerator filters and boiling. Boiling actually concentrates PFAS slightly, because the chemicals do not break down at boiling point while the water volume shrinks. Our PFAS filtration guide covers testing and technology in more depth.

What water filter removes fluoride?

Reverse osmosis removes roughly 85 to 95 percent of fluoride and is the most practical whole diet option. Activated alumina and bone char filters also work and are used in dedicated fluoride cartridges. Regular activated carbon does not remove fluoride at all, which means standard pitcher filters, faucet attachments and refrigerator filters leave it untouched. Check the certification rather than the marketing, because fluoride reduction is one of the most commonly overstated claims in the category.

Does a Brita filter remove lead, microplastics or fluoride?

Partly, no, and no. Brita's Elite and Longlast filters are certified to reduce lead under NSF/ANSI 53, so lead is genuinely covered on those specific models, though not on the standard filter. Microplastics are not covered: no Brita filter carries NSF/ANSI 401 certification for microplastic reduction. Fluoride is not removed by any Brita filter, because activated carbon does not adsorb it. Brita's own published testing also shows the standard and Elite filters do not significantly reduce PFOA or PFOS.

Are Berkey filters still worth buying in 2026?

We would not buy one right now. The EPA classified the original Black Berkey elements as pesticide devices under FIFRA, and those elements remain off the US market while Berkey International's appeal works through the First Circuit. The replacement Phoenix elements introduced in late 2025 carry NSF/ANSI 42 and 372 certification only, which covers taste, odor and lead free materials, not the broad contaminant reduction the original elements were marketed on. A separate consumer class action over those marketing claims is still unresolved. A certified reverse osmosis system does more, with paperwork you can check.

Do I need a whole house filter or is a kitchen filter enough?

For most people a kitchen filter is enough, because drinking and cooking water is where the dose is. A whole house system earns its keep in three situations: chlorine or chloramine heavy water that irritates skin and hair, well water with sediment or iron that damages fixtures, or a documented lead service line where you want every tap covered. Whole house carbon is excellent for chlorine and sediment but usually lacks the contact time to handle PFAS at parts per trillion, so the standard setup is whole house for volume plus a certified drinking water system at the kitchen sink.

Does reverse osmosis strip out healthy minerals?

Yes, reverse osmosis removes dissolved minerals along with contaminants. In practice this matters far less than it is made to sound, because drinking water is a minor mineral source next to food. If it concerns you, the Bluevua RO100ROPOT adds a remineralization stage that puts calcium and magnesium back after the membrane, AquaTru sells a separate alkaline version of the Glass Carafe that does the same, and six stage under sink systems such as the iSpring RCC7AK do the same.

Do refrigerator water filters do anything?

They improve taste and most are certified to reduce chlorine under NSF/ANSI 42, with some also certified for lead under NSF/ANSI 53. That is the whole story. Pore sizes sit in the 10 to 20 micron range, so they do nothing for microplastics or nanoplastics, and almost none carry PFAS certification. The bigger problem is behavioral: fridge filters are the cartridge people forget, and an expired filter can release accumulated contaminants back into the water.

How often should I replace a water filter?

Sediment and carbon pre filters run 6 to 12 months. Reverse osmosis membranes last 2 to 3 years. Certified carbon block pitchers run 2 to 6 months depending on volume, and standard pitcher cartridges are shorter still at around 40 gallons. Shower filters are typically 10,000 gallons or 6 months, and whole house carbon tanks run for years rather than months. Replace on the schedule rather than waiting for a taste change, because an expired filter is worse than no filter once accumulated contaminants begin releasing back into the water.

The Bottom Line

Filtering your water is the highest leverage change most households can make on chemical exposure, because it is the one contaminated thing you consume liters of every day and the one where the fix is entirely under your control. The regulatory timeline for PFAS now runs to 2031 and for lead pipes to 2037. A filter takes an afternoon.

Spend ten minutes on your Consumer Confidence Report and the EWG database before you spend anything else. If the answer is chlorine and taste, a certified carbon block is enough and costs very little. If lead, PFAS, nitrates or fluoride appear, you need reverse osmosis, and the Bluevua RO100ROPOT is the one we would buy. Every product here is listed in our store.
