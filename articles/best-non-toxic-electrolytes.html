Electrolyte powder went from a sports aisle afterthought to a daily habit in about four years. The category is now built on stick packs: a single serve sachet, torn open and tipped into a bottle, once or twice a day, every day. That is a product design decision with consequences, and almost nobody has looked at them.

Here is the uncomfortable part. An electrolyte powder is, by definition, ground up minerals and salt. Minerals are mined, and mined minerals carry lead. Salt, if it comes from the sea, carries plastic. And the whole thing ships in a polyethylene lined pouch designed to be thrown away after ninety seconds of use. Every structural feature of this category points at exactly the contaminants we cover on this site, and the brands selling it have built their marketing on the word clean.

This guide covers what independent laboratories have actually measured in electrolyte powders, what nobody has measured, which specific brands the testing flagged, and the small set we would actually drink. It sits alongside our guides to microplastics in salt and pantry staples, microplastics in supplements, and heavy metals in protein powder, because the same physics runs through all three.

1. Nobody Tests Electrolyte Powder for Microplastics

We will start with the honest answer, because a lot of pages on this topic will not give it to you. There is no published microplastic particle count for any electrolyte powder. No brand commissions that testing. No watchdog has run the category. Mamavation, Clean Label Project, Consumer Reports, ConsumerLab, and Lead Safe Mama have all touched electrolytes or adjacent powders, and every one of them tested for PFAS, heavy metals, or label accuracy. None of them counted plastic particles.

So if you see a hydration brand advertising itself as microplastic free, it is a claim with nothing behind it. There is no test to pass, and nobody is checking.

What We Can Say Instead
The absence of powder specific data does not leave us guessing. Microplastic contamination in beverages has been measured directly, salt contamination has been measured directly, and packaging construction is a matter of public record. Put those together and you can rank the plastic sources in a finished electrolyte drink from largest to smallest, which is more useful than a particle count for the powder alone would be.

The most comprehensive recent measurement is a 2025 University of Birmingham study in Science of the Total Environment, which analyzed seven categories of hot and cold drinks from the UK market. It found microplastics in 100 percent of beverages tested, at levels from around 17 particles per liter in soft drinks to 60 per liter in hot tea, and estimated average intake from drinks alone at 1.65 particles per kilogram of body weight per day. Juices and energy drinks in plastic bottles carried more than the same drinks in paper or aluminum packaging.

That last detail is the one that matters for electrolytes. The drink is mostly water, and the container is mostly the story.

2. The Four Places Plastic Enters Your Electrolyte Drink

Ranked by how much they actually contribute, and how much control you have over each.

Where the Plastic in Your Electrolyte Drink Comes From

The water is the whole ball game

A 2024 Columbia and Rutgers study published in PNAS found roughly 240,000 nanoplastic particles per liter in bottled water, ten to a hundred times more than earlier microplastic counts had suggested. If you are tearing open a clean organic electrolyte stick and pouring it into a single use plastic bottle of water, the powder is a rounding error next to the water. Filtering your own water solves more of this problem than any powder switch can. Our guides to filtering PFAS and microplastics and whether nanoplastics can be filtered at all cover what actually works.

The vessel is the second lever

Electrolyte drinks are acidic by design, usually built on citric acid or citrate salts, and they get shaken. Acidity, agitation, and warmth are the three conditions that increase shedding from plastic surfaces. A powder mixed in a stainless or glass bottle avoids that entirely, which is why our water bottle guide holds a hard line on no plastic anywhere in the drink path.

The Glass Bottle Surprise
If you buy ready to drink electrolyte beverages instead of powder, do not assume glass is automatically safer. A June 2025 study from ANSES, France's food safety agency, published in the Journal of Food Composition and Analysis, found that soft drinks, iced tea, lemonade, and beer in glass bottles averaged around 100 microplastic particles per liter, against 2 to 30 in plastic bottles and cans. The source was not the glass. It was the polyester based paint on the metal caps, which sheds flakes as the caps scuff in transit. The agency showed that blowing and rinsing the caps before capping cut contamination by about 60 percent, which no manufacturer is required to do.

The stick pack, in proportion

Single serve electrolyte sachets are usually a three or four layer laminate: a polyester outer film for print and strength, a micro thin aluminum layer for the moisture and light barrier, and an inner sealing layer of polyethylene or linear low density polyethylene. That inner polyethylene film is what your powder actually sits against.

We want to be proportionate here. Dry powder, at room temperature, in short term contact with polyethylene, is the lowest risk migration scenario that exists in food packaging. This is not a hot fatty food against a PVC gasket, which is the situation that genuinely produces high plasticizer numbers, as we covered in the plastic in your jar lid. Nobody should panic about a sachet.

But there is a volume argument. A daily stick pack habit is 365 individually sealed plastic and aluminum laminates a year, each with a large surface area relative to six grams of powder. The same product bought as a bulk bag or canister reduces that to a handful of plastic to powder interfaces a year. That is the change worth making, and it is usually cheaper too.

3. The Sea Salt Paradox

This is the finding that reorganized how we think about the category.

Nearly every premium electrolyte brand names its salt as a selling point. Unrefined sea salt. Celtic salt. Redmond Real Salt. Pink Himalayan. The implication is that this salt is more natural, less processed, and therefore cleaner than the refined white stuff.

On the two contaminants this site cares about, the opposite is true.

Unrefined Salt Is the Dirtier Salt on Both Axes

Sea salt is evaporated from surface seawater, and surface seawater is where the plastic is. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why sea salt consistently returns the highest microplastic counts of any food grade salt in the peer reviewed literature. Mined and underground brine salts, which never touch the modern ocean, test far lower. We laid out the full salt by salt picture in our salt and pantry guide.

The heavy metals run the same direction. The trace mineral content that unrefined salts advertise is, chemically speaking, the contamination. You cannot keep the interesting minerals and drop the lead, because they arrive together from the same rock. Independent lab testing has measured Redmond Real Salt at roughly 250 to 290 parts per billion lead and 36 to 91 ppb arsenic, and a February 2025 proposed class action against Celtic Ocean International cited testing around 460 ppb lead and 140 ppb arsenic in Celtic varieties.

Keep the Dose in Mind
An electrolyte serving contains a fraction of a gram of salt, so the contribution from the salt alone is small either way. We are not telling you a stick pack will poison you. We are pointing out that the ingredient marketed as the clean, natural, mineral rich part of the formula is the part carrying both of the contaminants this site exists to track, and that the plain refined version genuinely does better on both.

4. What the PFAS Testing Found

In June 2023, Mamavation sent 40 powdered electrolyte products from 30 brands to an EPA certified laboratory for total fluorine, measured by oxygen flask combustion and ion selective electrode with a detection limit of 10 parts per million. Detectable organic fluorine is a screening indicator for PFAS forever chemicals. It does not identify specific compounds, and it can also pick up other organofluorines, so a detection is a flag to investigate rather than a conviction.

Nine of the 40 products, 23 percent, came back with detectable fluorine between 10 and 105 ppm. The pattern of which ones is the interesting part.

5. Why Every Mineral Powder Carries Lead

Electrolytes are minerals. Minerals come out of rock, brine, or seawater, and every one of those sources naturally contains trace lead, cadmium, and arsenic alongside the sodium, potassium, magnesium, and calcium you wanted. It is the same reason lead rides along with the zinc in zinc oxide diaper creams and with the plant matter in protein powder. There is no clean version of a mined mineral, only lower and higher numbers.

California's Proposition 65 sets the daily lead exposure threshold that triggers a warning label at 0.5 micrograms. That is a deliberately conservative number, far stricter than federal limits, and mineral heavy supplements trip it routinely. The result is a steady drumbeat of Proposition 65 notices against this category.

The LMNT lead numbers, honestly

LMNT is the biggest name in this category and the one readers ask about most, so it deserves the full story rather than a headline.

In November 2024, Lead Safe Mama had LMNT Raw Unflavored tested by an independent laboratory. The result was 27 parts per billion lead, with cadmium, arsenic, and mercury all non detect. Tamara Rubin's position is that a supplement claiming health benefits should not test positive for lead at any level, and she noted the result sits above the 10 ppb action level proposed in the 2021 Baby Food Safety Act.

In absolute terms, 27 ppb works out to roughly 0.16 micrograms of lead in a 6 gram packet. That is below California's 0.5 microgram daily threshold for a single serving, and well below the federal interim reference levels of 2.2 micrograms a day for children and 8.8 for women of childbearing age. Both of those facts are true at once, and which one you weight is a judgement call rather than a scientific one.

What is not a judgement call is what happened next. The health scoring app Oasis published a claim that LMNT contained seven times the legal lead limit. That figure was wrong, the app acknowledged a calculation error had inflated it, and LMNT sued Oasis in 2026 over the claim and the delay in retracting it. We include this because the same reflex that makes a site like ours useful, taking contamination seriously, also makes it easy to amplify a number nobody checked. The 27 ppb result is real. The seven times figure was not.

8. Brands That Need Caution

None of these are products we would tell you to throw away. They are products where something specific and documented did not clear our bar, and you should know what it is.

LMNT

Prop 65 consent judgment, 27 ppb lead, three active class actions

The category leader, and non detect for fluorine in Mamavation's screen. But it settled a Proposition 65 lead action over its Recharge Orange Salt and Chocolate Salt mixes in 2021, its Raw Unflavored tested at 27 ppb lead in November 2024 independent testing, and three separate 2025 class actions allege that 300 to 450 mg of maltodextrin per serving hides behind a natural flavors listing in a product marketed as all natural, keto, and paleo.

To be fair to LMNT: the lead figure is small in absolute terms, and the widely shared claim that it carried seven times the legal limit came from a health app that conceded a calculation error. LMNT sued over it in 2026. Our issue is the maltodextrin labeling and the sodium load, at 1,000 mg per stick, which is a lot for daily use if you are not actually sweating it out.

Redmond Re-Lyte

Built on a salt that tests high for lead and arsenic

Re-Lyte returned non detect for fluorine and has a genuinely short ingredient list, which is why it appears in a lot of clean roundups. The problem is upstream. Its mineral base is Redmond Real Salt, which independent laboratory testing has measured at roughly 250 to 290 parts per billion lead and 36 to 91 ppb arsenic. We do not recommend Real Salt as a daily cooking salt in our salt guide, and we cannot recommend it as a daily supplement either without contradicting ourselves.

Skratch Labs

Proposition 65 settlement covering lead, mercury, and PFOA

Skratch has one of the better formulas in sports nutrition, real fruit and cane sugar with nothing synthetic, and its Sport Hydration mix was non detect for fluorine. But the Environmental Research Center filed a Proposition 65 notice in May 2022 and a complaint in September 2022 alleging lead, mercury, and PFOA in Skratch dietary supplements, settled in August 2023 with reformulation limits. Proposition 65 thresholds are strict and this plaintiff files frequently, so read it as a yellow flag rather than a scandal. It is still a settled action naming the brand's supplements on three chemicals including a PFAS compound, which is more than any of our picks carries.

Ultima Replenisher

Class action over DL malic acid behind a naturally flavored claim

Non detect for fluorine, zero sugar, stevia sweetened, and a decent mineral spread. A California class action alleges that its raspberry and watermelon flavors contain DL malic acid, the synthetic form extracted from petrochemicals, while the packaging claims natural flavoring. On a site about avoiding petrochemical derived ingredients, that is exactly the claim we cannot wave through.

FlavCity, Paleovalley, Jigsaw Health, Plexus, Trace Minerals PowerPak

Detectable fluorine in independent screening

All five returned detectable total fluorine in Mamavation's 2023 lab work: FlavCity up to 277 ppm across repeat tests, Jigsaw Health 89 ppm, Paleovalley 47 to 55 ppm across two flavors, Trace Minerals PowerPak 23 ppm, and Plexus Hydrate 17 and 10 ppm on two rounds. Total fluorine is a screen rather than a specific PFAS identification, and Nuun and two others detected once then came back non detect, so a single hit is not proof. But FlavCity and Paleovalley in particular are sold on clean label credibility, and these were the highest results in the study.

Liquid I.V.

Stacked false advertising suits

Non detect for fluorine, and genuinely effective at what it does. It also carries a run of consumer class actions: an August 2026 suit alleging its Sugar Free line is sweetened with 4 to 5 grams of allulose listed first by weight, an earlier suit over preservative free labeling, and one over slack filled packets. None of these are contamination claims. They are a pattern of the label not matching the pack, which is enough to keep it off our list when cleaner options exist.

Gatorade, Powerade, Prime and the ready to drink aisle

Artificial colors, and the bottle is the bigger problem

Gatorade Zero powder was non detect for fluorine, so this is not a contamination call. It is that petroleum derived dyes have no function in a daily drink, and that buying electrolytes pre mixed in a PET bottle reintroduces the single largest plastic source in this entire article. If you want a ready to drink option, the powder in your own steel bottle beats every bottled version on both counts.

9. Make It Yourself: The Zero Added Plastic Option

The cheapest and cleanest electrolyte drink has no packaging to test, no proprietary blend, and no brand claims to verify. If you are drinking electrolytes daily for general hydration rather than for athletic performance or illness, this covers it.

The Everyday Electrolyte Drink, Per Litre

Use Diamond Crystal kosher salt for the sodium, since it comes from underground brine rather than seawater and has tested non detect for heavy metals across multiple independent rounds. For magnesium, if you want it, Thorne Magnesium Bisglycinate powder is NSF Certified for Sport, which means per batch contaminant testing, and it dissolves cleanly. Mix into filtered water and drink it out of glass or stainless.

A word on sugar. Commercial rehydration formulas include glucose on purpose, because the sodium glucose cotransporter mechanism moves sodium and water across the gut wall faster together than sodium alone. That matters when you are genuinely depleted, from illness or hours of hard sweating, which is why we recommend a real oral rehydration solution like TRIORAL for those situations rather than the homemade version. For ordinary daily hydration you do not need it.

One Genuine Safety Note
Potassium supplementation is the one place in this article where more is not harmless. If you take an ACE inhibitor, an angiotensin receptor blocker, a potassium sparing diuretic like spironolactone, or you have reduced kidney function, added potassium can be dangerous. Check with your doctor before using potassium chloride salt substitutes or high potassium electrolyte powders. High sodium formulas like LMNT, at 1,000 mg a stick, are also worth discussing with a clinician if you have high blood pressure.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the cleanest electrolyte powder?

Our top pick is GOODONYA Organic Hydration, a six ingredient certified organic powder that returned non detect for total fluorine in Mamavation's independent lab screen, carries BioChecked Glyphosate Residue Free certification, and is sold as a single bulk bag rather than dozens of individual stick packs. Cure Hydration is the best everyday alternative and publishes per batch heavy metals results. For illness, heat, or anyone who wants the shortest possible ingredient list, TRIORAL oral rehydration salts use the World Health Organization low osmolarity formula with four pharmaceutical grade ingredients, no flavors, no sweeteners, and no sea salt.

Do electrolyte powders contain microplastics?

Nobody has published microplastic particle counts for electrolyte powder, and no brand tests for it. What we do know is where the plastic in a finished electrolyte drink comes from: the water you mix it into, the bottle or shaker you drink it from, the unrefined sea salt in the formula, and the stick pack itself, whose innermost layer is polyethylene film pressed against the powder. A 2025 University of Birmingham study found microplastics in 100 percent of the beverages it tested, with plastic bottled drinks higher than paper or aluminum packaged ones. Filtering your water and using a glass or steel vessel does far more than switching powders.

Do electrolyte powders have PFAS?

Some do. In June 2023 Mamavation sent 40 electrolyte products from 30 brands to an EPA certified laboratory for total fluorine, an indicator of PFAS forever chemicals. Nine of the 40, or 23 percent, came back with detectable fluorine between 10 and 105 parts per million. The highest results were in brands marketed as clean: FlavCity Electrolyte Drink Mix returned readings up to 277 ppm across repeat tests, Jigsaw Health Adrenal Cocktail 89 ppm, and Paleovalley Essential Electrolytes 47 to 55 ppm. Nuun Sport and two others detected once and came back non detect on retest. Total fluorine is a screening signal, not a specific PFAS identification.

Does LMNT contain lead?

In November 2024 Lead Safe Mama had LMNT Raw Unflavored tested by an independent laboratory and reported 27 parts per billion lead, with cadmium, arsenic, and mercury all non detect. That is a small number in absolute terms, roughly 0.16 micrograms in a 6 gram packet, well under federal interim reference levels, but above California's Proposition 65 daily lead threshold of 0.5 micrograms if you drink several. LMNT also settled a 2021 Proposition 65 action brought by the Environmental Research Center over lead in its Recharge Orange Salt and Chocolate Salt mixes, agreeing to an injunction capping daily lead exposure at 0.5 micrograms without a warning label. Separately, LMNT sued the health scoring app Oasis in 2026 after Oasis claimed the powder carried seven times the legal lead limit, a figure the app conceded was inflated by a calculation error.

Why do electrolyte powders test positive for lead at all?

Because electrolytes are minerals, and minerals are mined. Sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, and zinc are all extracted from rock, brine, or seawater, and every one of those sources naturally carries trace lead, cadmium, and arsenic. The same physics that puts lead in zinc oxide and in protein powder puts it in a hydration stick. California's Proposition 65 threshold of 0.5 micrograms of lead a day is strict enough that mineral heavy supplements trip it routinely, which is why 2026 has produced a steady run of Proposition 65 lead notices against electrolyte, greens, collagen, and amino acid powders. Trace detection is normal for the category. What separates brands is how much, and whether they publish the number.

Is the sea salt in electrolyte powder a microplastic problem?

Sea salt is the most microplastic contaminated food grade salt in the published literature, because it is evaporated from surface seawater that already contains plastic particles. Mined and underground brine salts test far lower. That creates an awkward result for this category: the powders that market unrefined sea salt, Celtic salt, or pink Himalayan salt as a purity feature are using the salt most likely to carry both particles and heavy metals, while the clinical formulas built on refined sodium chloride and potassium chloride avoid both. Redmond Re-Lyte is a clear example, since it is built on Redmond Real Salt, which independent labs have measured at roughly 250 to 290 ppb lead and 36 to 91 ppb arsenic.

Are electrolyte stick packs bad for you?

A single serve stick pack is typically a laminate of polyester film, a micro thin aluminum barrier, and an inner sealing layer of polyethylene, and the polyethylene is the layer in direct contact with your powder. That contact is dry, short, and at room temperature, which is the lowest risk migration scenario there is, so we would not call a stick pack dangerous. But it is a lot of plastic surface area per gram of product, repeated every single day, and a bulk bag or canister cuts the number of plastic to powder interfaces from one per serving to one per month. If you drink electrolytes daily, buying in bulk is the meaningful change.

Do I actually need an electrolyte powder?

Most people eating normally and drinking to thirst do not. Electrolyte supplements earn their place in specific situations: heavy sweating over an hour or more, hot weather work, vomiting or diarrhea, fasting, low carbohydrate diets that flush sodium, and some medications. Outside those, a pinch of well chosen salt in water with a squeeze of citrus covers the same ground for pennies, with no packaging, no sweetener, and no mineral blend to test. Buying a daily hydration stick you do not need is the most avoidable exposure in this entire article.

Is a plastic shaker bottle a problem for electrolyte drinks?

It is the second largest plastic source in the drink, after the water itself. Electrolyte mixes are acidic by design, built on citric acid or citrate salts, and shaking adds mechanical abrasion. Acidity, agitation, and warmth are the three conditions that increase shedding from plastic surfaces, and a shaker combines all three. Mixing in a stainless steel or glass bottle removes that variable completely, which is why our drinkware standard requires no plastic anywhere in the drink path.
