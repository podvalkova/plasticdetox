1. Two Tests, Not One

Almost every water filter roundup asks one question: what does it remove? That is the right first question, and most filters fail it badly. But it is only half the job.

Here is the part that gets skipped. A filter can strip 99.9 percent of plastic particles out of your tap water and then hold that water in a polypropylene bladder for six hours before you pour it through a plastic spout into a plastic tumbler. You have paid several hundred dollars to remove plastic from water, and then handed the water back to plastic at every step after the membrane.

We rank on both. First, does the technology physically catch particles below one micron and strip PFAS. Second, once the water is clean, what does it touch before it reaches your glass. A filter that wins the first test and loses the second is doing part of a job.

2. What Actually Removes Microplastics and PFAS

This comes down to pore size, and the numbers are not close.

Microplastics are defined as particles from 1 micron up to 5 millimetres. Nanoplastics sit below 1 micron, and they are the fraction that matters most by count. When Columbia University researchers applied laser microscopy to bottled water in 2024, roughly 90 percent of the particles they found were nanoplastics rather than microplastics. Any filter that stops at the microplastic threshold is missing the majority of what is actually in the water.

A standard pitcher filter has pores in the range of tens of microns. A nanoplastic particle passing through it is closer in scale to a fly through an open garage door than a sieve. That is not a knock on the product design, it is simply what those filters were built to do: improve taste by adsorbing chlorine. They were never particle filters.

PFAS behave differently again. They are dissolved chemicals rather than particles, so pore size alone does not decide the outcome. Reverse osmosis rejects them at the membrane. Some carbon blocks adsorb them well enough to earn NSF P473 certification for PFOA and PFOS. Most filters do neither.

3. The Test Nobody Runs: What Your Water Touches Next

Once water leaves the membrane it has to go somewhere, and the design choices there vary enormously.

Under sink systems store water in a bladder tank. A standard reverse osmosis tank is a steel shell with an internal bladder that keeps the water off the metal. On the widely sold APEC tanks that bladder is butyl with polypropylene components. It is inert, food grade and not a safety problem in any conventional sense, but it does mean your filtered water sits in a polymer bladder under pressure, sometimes for hours, before you draw a glass.

Tankless systems skip the storage step. A tankless design filters on demand and sends water straight to the faucet, so there is no reservoir at all. That is the cleanest water path available on a plumbed system.

Countertop systems vary the most. Some hold filtered water in a plastic carafe. A few use borosilicate glass. That single design choice is the difference between a countertop unit that finishes the job and one that undoes part of it, and it costs the manufacturer very little either way.

Pitchers are almost universally plastic. The body, the lid, the filter housing and the spout are usually all plastic, which means filtered water is in contact with a polymer surface from the moment it is filtered until the moment you drink it. Glass bodied pitchers exist but are rare.

None of this makes an under sink system a bad buy. It is a tradeoff, and a butyl bladder is a much smaller exposure than unfiltered tap water. But you should know it is there, because no manufacturer will tell you.

4. How We Chose

Every product here cleared the same five checks we run before anything gets recommended:

  1. Filtration performance. Does the technology physically remove sub micron particles and PFAS, not just improve taste.
  2. Materials in the water path. What the filtered water contacts between the membrane and your glass.
  3. Independent testing and certification. WQA or IAPMO certification against the relevant NSF/ANSI standards, or third party lab results.
  4. Lawsuits. We checked for class actions and consumer protection actions against each brand.
  5. Recalls. We checked for open recalls on every model listed.

Among the systems that passed all five, we let owner reviews break the tie. Certification tells you a unit performed in a lab. Several thousand owners tell you whether it still works in a kitchen after a year.

7. What Does Not Work

Standard pitcher and faucet filters. Built for chlorine taste. Pore sizes in the tens of microns, no meaningful PFAS reduction, and filtered water held in plastic. A useful taste upgrade, not a contaminant filter.

Refrigerator filters. Same story, with the added problem that people rarely change them on schedule.

Bottled water. The 2024 Columbia study put roughly 240,000 particles per liter in popular brands, about 90 percent nanoplastics. PET bottles shed under heat and time, so a case sitting in a warm garage is worse again. We cover this in detail in microplastics in bottled water.

Boiling, mostly. A 2024 study found that boiling hard water forms limescale that traps plastic particles, allowing removal up to around 90 percent when the water is calcium rich. In soft water the effect largely disappears. Boiling does nothing at all for PFAS, and since it reduces the water volume it can slightly concentrate them.

Alkaline and mineral drops. No particle filtration whatsoever.

8. Reading the Certifications

Filter marketing leans hard on numbers that sound official and are not. These are the ones that mean something:

  • NSF/ANSI 401. The standard covering incidental contaminants, and the one under which microplastic reduction in the 0.5 to 1 micron range is verified. This is what to look for on any microplastics claim. It does not cover nanoplastics.
  • NSF/ANSI 58. Reverse osmosis system performance.
  • NSF/ANSI 53. Health effects, including lead and cysts.
  • NSF/ANSI 42. Aesthetic effects, meaning chlorine, taste and odor. On its own this tells you nothing about plastic or PFAS.
  • NSF/ANSI 372. Lead free contact materials. A materials standard, not a performance one. A filter certified only to 372 has been checked for what it is made of, not for what it removes.
  • NSF P473. PFOA and PFOS reduction specifically. The one to look for on PFAS claims.

Certification is also issued by different bodies. WQA and IAPMO both certify against NSF/ANSI standards, and either is legitimate. What is not legitimate is the very common phrase "tested to NSF standards", which appears on a great many product pages and means the manufacturer ran a test itself. Certified and tested to are different claims.

9. Filters That Need Caution

Not a microplastics filter

Standard Brita and PUR pitchers

Widely assumed to filter plastic. Standard models are certified under NSF/ANSI 42 and 53 for taste and some heavy metals, with pore sizes far too large for nanoplastics, and they hold filtered water in plastic. Some upgraded models in both ranges do carry NSF/ANSI 401 certification, so check the exact model number rather than trusting the brand.

Filtered water stored in plastic

Countertop systems with plastic carafes

Several well reviewed countertop reverse osmosis units filter to the same standard as our picks and then hold the result in a plastic reservoir. The filtration is fine. If you are buying specifically to reduce plastic exposure, the storage material is not a detail.

Under sink housings and leaks

Under sink systems generally

There is an active consumer class action investigation into under sink water filter housings that can crack, leak and cause property damage. It is a category wide investigation rather than an action against any of our picks, but check your fittings after installation and again after a week, and do not install over an unprotected cabinet base.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best water filter for microplastics and PFAS?

Reverse osmosis is the only widely available household technology that removes both reliably, because its membrane pores are roughly 0.0001 microns, far smaller than any microplastic or nanoplastic particle, and it also rejects PFAS. Our top pick is the Bluevua RO100ROPOT countertop system, which pairs reverse osmosis with a borosilicate glass carafe so the filtered water never sits in plastic, and needs no plumbing. If you want the strongest third party certification instead, the AquaTru Glass Carafe is certified by IAPMO to NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 58, 401 and 372.

Do Brita and PUR pitchers remove microplastics?

Standard Brita and PUR pitcher filters are built to reduce chlorine taste, odor and some heavy metals. Their pore sizes are far too large to stop nanoplastics, and most standard models are not certified under NSF/ANSI 401 for microplastic reduction. Some upgraded models within those ranges do carry 401 certification, so check the specific model rather than the brand. None of them remove PFAS to the degree reverse osmosis does, and every one of them pours filtered water through a plastic housing.

Is there a water filter pitcher that is not made of plastic?

Yes. The LifeStraw Home 7 Cup Glass pitcher uses a borosilicate glass body with a silicone base, and is certified to NSF/ANSI 401 for microplastic reduction at 99.999 percent and to NSF P473 for PFOA and PFOS reduction above 99 percent. The filter membrane itself is polysulfone, so it is not entirely plastic free, but the vessel holding your filtered water is glass. Its main tradeoff is flow rate, since filling the pitcher takes roughly 20 minutes.

Does boiling water remove microplastics?

Boiling can help in hard water. A 2024 study found that boiling calcium rich tap water encourages limescale to form around plastic particles, trapping them so they can be strained out, with removals reported up to around 90 percent in very hard water. In soft water the effect is far weaker. Boiling also does nothing for PFAS. Because PFAS do not break down at boiling point and the water volume shrinks, boiling can slightly concentrate them.

Do I need a filter if I drink bottled water?

Bottled water is a poor substitute. A 2024 Columbia University study using laser microscopy found roughly 240,000 plastic particles per liter in popular bottled water brands, around 90 percent of them nanoplastics, which is far more than typical treated tap water. Filtering your own tap water at home and storing it in glass or stainless steel costs less per liter and gives you a lower particle count.

What does NSF/ANSI 401 certification mean?

NSF/ANSI 401 covers what the standard calls incidental contaminants, and it is the standard under which microplastic reduction down to the 0.5 to 1 micron range is verified. It is the certification to look for on any filter making a microplastics claim. It does not cover nanoplastics, which are smaller than 1 micron, and it is separate from NSF/ANSI 53 for lead and cysts, 58 for reverse osmosis systems and 372 for lead free contact materials.

Does reverse osmosis strip out good minerals?

Yes, reverse osmosis removes dissolved minerals along with contaminants. In practice this matters far less than it is made to sound, because drinking water is a minor mineral source next to food. If it concerns you, several systems including the Bluevua and the AquaTru Glass Carafe add a remineralization stage that puts calcium and magnesium back after the membrane.

How often do filters need replacing?

Sediment and carbon pre filters typically run 6 to 12 months. Reverse osmosis membranes usually last 2 to 3 years. Pitcher filters are the shortest lived, often 2 to 4 months depending on volume. An expired filter is worse than no filter, because accumulated contaminants can begin releasing back into the water, so set a reminder rather than waiting for a taste change.

The Bottom Line

Filtering water is the highest leverage change most households can make on plastic exposure, because it is the one contaminated thing you consume liters of every day. Reverse osmosis is the technology that works. Beyond that, the only question left is whether the filter hands your clean water back to plastic before you drink it, and that question has a straightforward answer if you look for a glass carafe.

For most people, the Bluevua RO100ROPOT is the one to buy. Every product here is listed in our store.
