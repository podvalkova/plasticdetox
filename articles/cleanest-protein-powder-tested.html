Protein powder is the most contaminated supplement category with real published data behind that claim, and it is also the category with the largest testing blind spot. Two independent programs have screened it for heavy metals. Neither tested for the plastic the powder ships in. This guide walks through exactly what the testing found, which brands were flagged in the 2025 lawsuit wave, and the cleanest realistic picks that publish their per batch numbers so you can read the lead and cadmium content yourself.

This is the deep dive companion to our broader Supplements and Microplastics guide, which ranks every supplement form from cleanest to dirtiest. Here we focus entirely on protein.

Why Protein Powder Is the Riskiest Category

Three things stack up in protein powder that do not stack up in a vitamin D capsule. First, the dose is large. You take grams of powder per serving, often twice a day, not micrograms of an active. Whatever contaminant is present rides along at that volume. Second, many of the base ingredients are plant derived or cocoa based, and both pull heavy metals out of the soil they grew in. Third, the packaging is a large plastic tub or a single use pouch with high plastic surface area, and the powder sits against it for months.

Put those together and protein powder becomes the one supplement where the daily ingested load of both heavy metals and plastic contact is highest. That is why it draws the most independent testing, and the most litigation.

What the Testing Actually Found

Direct evidenceTwo bodies of testing matter here.

Clean Label Project tested 160 protein powders across 258 contaminants in 2023 and 2024. The headline finding was lead: 65 percent of chocolate powders, 28 percent of whey powders, and 26 percent of collagen powders exceeded California Prop 65 thresholds for lead. The often quoted composite figure, 47 percent exceeding at least one Prop 65 threshold, is weighted heavily by lead. Cadmium was the secondary story. Chocolate flavors carried up to 110 times more cadmium than vanilla, and plant based powders about 5 times more cadmium than whey. The Council for Responsible Nutrition publicly disputed the methodology in January 2025, arguing Prop 65 thresholds are more conservative than federal limits. The lead findings hold regardless of that dispute.

Consumer Reports tested 23 protein powders and shakes in October 2025 and found more than two thirds exceeded California's daily lead safety threshold. Plant based and meal replacement style powders were disproportionately represented at the top of the range. Those findings triggered the class action wave detailed below.

Prop 65 is a conservative threshold, and that is the point
California's Maximum Allowable Dose Level for lead is 0.5 micrograms per day, stricter than federal food limits. Critics are right that exceeding it is not the same as an acute hazard. But lead has no known safe level, it accumulates, and a daily supplement is exactly the kind of repeated exposure the threshold was designed to catch. We treat Prop 65 exceedance as a real signal, not a technicality.

What to Skip

  • Chocolate plant based powders in plastic tubs. The worst combination: cocoa cadmium, plant lead, plus high plastic surface contact.
  • Mass gainers and "complete meal" powders. Multiple flagged in the 2025 CR testing at the highest lead levels.
  • Any brand with active heavy metals litigation on any SKU. For a clean recommendation we do not split hairs between a brand's affected and unaffected products.
  • "Proprietary blend" labels with no per batch COA. If you cannot read the lead and cadmium numbers for your lot, you are trusting a marketing claim.
  • Single serve pouches bought in bulk. Maximum plastic surface area per gram of powder, and no published contaminant data.
"Organic" does not mean low metal
Organic certification restricts synthetic pesticides and fertilizers. It says nothing about the lead and cadmium already in the soil. Some of the highest cadmium results in the Clean Label Project screen were certified organic plant powders. For metals, prefer third party metal tested over organic.

How to Reduce Your Exposure

  1. Default to unflavored whey isolate. Lowest tested metals, and you control the flavor with fruit or unsweetened cocoa if you want it.
  2. Buy from a brand that publishes per batch COAs. A lot number lookup beats any certification badge because you read the real numbers.
  3. Rotate your protein sources. Alternating powders, and getting some protein from whole food, keeps any single contaminant from accumulating.
  4. Transfer to glass at home. Move the powder into a clean airtight glass jar and store it cool, dark, and dry. Do not leave it against the plastic tub for months, and do not store it in a humid cabinet.
  5. Do not over scoop. More servings means proportionally more of whatever metal is present. Use the protein you actually need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which protein powders have the least heavy metals?

The cleanest results come from single ingredient, unflavored whey isolates from brands that publish per batch certificates of analysis. Transparent Labs Grass Fed Whey Isolate publishes per batch COAs with a public lot lookup and is Informed Protein certified. As a category, whey tested lower for lead and cadmium than chocolate flavored and plant based powders. Clean Label Project found 65 percent of chocolate powders, 28 percent of whey powders, and 26 percent of collagen powders over the Prop 65 lead threshold.

Why do plant based and chocolate powders test higher for lead and cadmium?

Plants pull heavy metals from soil, so pea, rice, and hemp proteins concentrate whatever lead and cadmium were in the growing soil. Cocoa is a known cadmium accumulator, which is why chocolate flavors tested up to 110 times higher for cadmium than vanilla and plant based tested about 5 times higher than whey. Organic certification does not lower this, because it does not change the metal content of the soil.

Is protein powder tested for microplastics?

No. Clean Label Project, Consumer Reports, Mamavation, NSF Certified for Sport, and Informed Sport all test for heavy metals and other contaminants, but none test the powder or the plastic tub for microplastics. Powder in large tubs and single use pouches has high plastic surface contact and nobody is measuring what sheds into it. Clean for heavy metals does not mean clean for plastic, which is why we still recommend transferring to glass at home.

Did Consumer Reports really find lead in protein powder?

Yes. An October 2025 Consumer Reports investigation tested 23 protein powders and shakes and found more than two thirds exceeded California's daily lead threshold. The findings triggered class action lawsuits against brands including Naked Nutrition, Huel, Garden of Life, and OWYN. These are Consumer Reports findings and filed allegations, not trial verdicts. Plant based and meal replacement style powders were disproportionately represented among the highest results.

Does NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport mean a powder is low in heavy metals?

Those programs test every batch for banned substances and contaminants and are a real quality signal, but they certify against sport doping and safety thresholds, not against California's stricter Prop 65 daily lead limit. A powder can carry the badge and still exceed Prop 65 for lead. The strongest signal is a brand that publishes its actual per batch certificate of analysis with a lot number you can look up.

How do I reduce heavy metals and plastic from protein powder?

Choose single ingredient unflavored whey isolate, buy from a brand that publishes per batch COAs, and rotate protein sources so no single contaminant accumulates. Transfer the powder into a clean glass jar with an airtight lid, store it cool, dark, and dry, and do not exceed the serving you actually need.
