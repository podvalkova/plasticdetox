Formula Is a Regulated Category. Start There.

Most categories we cover are barely regulated. Formula is the opposite: it is one of the most tightly controlled foods in the American supply, with mandatory nutrient minimums, facility registration, and premarket notification to the FDA. In April 2026 the agency published the results of its largest formula testing program ever, over 300 samples collected from 2023 to 2025 and more than 120,000 data points: lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury all came in below EPA drinking water limits, 99 percent of samples had no detectable pesticides, and 25 of 30 PFAS compounds tested were never detected at all.

We lead with that because formula anxiety has real casualties. Parents scared off the regulated shelf have diluted formula, mixed homemade recipes from internet instructions, and bought unregistered imports, and each of those moves has hurt babies in documented cases. So the ground rules for everything that follows: every FDA registered formula is nutritionally complete, and feeding one, even the cheapest store brand, is always safer than diluting formula or making it at home. Never do either, and talk to your pediatrician before switching a formula that is working. Decisions about preterm and medically fragile infants belong to your medical team, full stop.

And still, registration is a floor, not a guarantee: the recall timeline later in this article happened entirely inside the regulated system, and independent testing keeps finding real differences between brands that all meet the same federal requirements. The FDA's surveillance answers the question is the supply broadly safe. It does not answer the question we get constantly from readers building a registry: if I am choosing anyway, which formula has the cleanest record? That question has a real answer, because two large independent testing rounds, a botulism outbreak, a toxin recall, and the largest settlement in formula litigation history all landed in the last eighteen months, and they did not land evenly.

The Four Checks We Run on Everything

Every verdict on this site, from the Product Check tool to the Top 100, comes from the same four checks. Formula is unusual in that all four carry real weight in one category:

The Four Point Formula Check

A featured pick has to clear all four. In most categories that bar eliminates a few brands. In formula it eliminated almost everyone.

Check 1: The Ingredient List

The carbohydrate in breast milk is lactose. It is also the most expensive carbohydrate a formula maker can buy, which is why many US formulas, especially sensitive, soy and budget varieties, replace some or all of it with corn syrup solids, glucose syrup solids or maltodextrin. European regulators require formula to be mostly lactose; the US sets no such rule. This is the single clearest label test you can run in the store aisle: read the first three ingredients and look for lactose, not a syrup.

The 5 Second Label Check

The fat blend is the second read. Palm oil or palm olein appears in many US formulas because it mimics one of breast milk's fatty acids, but clinical studies have repeatedly associated it with lower calcium and fat absorption and firmer stools, and several European style recipes simply leave it out. Then there is the DHA: the EU requires DHA in formula at 20 mg per 100 kcal or more, the US does not require it at all, and most brands that do add it extract it from algae with hexane, a petroleum solvent. Hexane free DHA exists; almost nobody uses it.

One ingredient level pattern from the 2026 testing deserves its own line: every plant based formula Consumer Reports tested was flagged for inorganic arsenic. Soy and rice ingredients concentrate arsenic from soil, and it shows up in the finished powder. Soy formula has legitimate medical uses, and if your pediatrician has prescribed it, follow your pediatrician. But nobody should choose plant based formula casually off the shelf, and the American Academy of Pediatrics says the same.

Check 2: The Packaging

Formula packaging has one genuinely good news story: BPA. For decades the epoxy lining of formula cans was a major BPA source, and formula sat against it for its entire shelf life. Industry abandoned BPA linings under pressure, the FDA formally removed BPA epoxy from approved formula packaging in 2013, and the testing now backs the change: in Consumer Reports' 2025 round, 40 of 41 formulas had no detectable BPA. The exception was Enfamil Nutramigen, the only formula where both BPA and acrylamide were detected, a finding Mead Johnson disputes. The caveat we always attach to BPA free claims applies here too: replacement linings are not disclosed, and some substitutes are chemical cousins of the thing they replaced. Our BPA free explainer covers why that matters.

The container format also matters, and here the data is newer. A 2023 peer reviewed study examined 13 varieties of infant milk powder for microplastics and traced part of the load to the packaging itself: an average of 42 particles per 100 grams of powder, with the highest counts in formulas sold in multilayer composite boxes and the lowest in cans. A formula fed infant swallows roughly 49 particles a day from the powder itself at those levels. Steel can beats composite box, and every formula we feature comes in one.

Two honest footnotes. First, the scoop: yes, it is plastic, and it touches the powder with every use. Keep using it, because the scoop is calibrated to the recipe and swapping it for a random spoon gets the ratio wrong, which is a real safety issue rather than a theoretical one. Measure with the scoop, stir with stainless steel. Second, ready to feed liquid: convenient, sterile, and sold in plastic bottles the formula sits against for months. Several ready to feed varieties also landed on Consumer Reports' concerning list in 2026. Powder in a steel can, prepared in glass, is the better default when your pediatrician has not specifically directed otherwise.

Read the flags precisely: CR's thresholds are deliberately stricter than federal requirements, no formula was found to violate an enforceable US limit, and trace lead appeared in everything because it appears in the whole food supply, breast milk included. The value of the testing is comparative. Some brands run consistently at the bottom of the range, and the same names kept landing in the Top Choices tier both years: every Bobbie formula tested, in both rounds, came back non detect or low for lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, BPA and acrylamide, and CR named Bobbie a top choice each time. Kendamil, HiPP Dutch Stage 1, Holle Bio Stage 1, Earth's Best Organic Dairy, Kirkland Signature ProCare and several Similac and Enfamil varieties, including 360 Total Care, Gentlease and NeuroPro, also tested clean. Fairness requires saying that plainly: on this one check, plenty of brands pass, including cheap store brands, and if budget is the constraint, a clean testing store brand is a genuinely good choice.

Above watchdog testing sits certification. The Clean Label Project Purity Award tests finished product off the shelf for hundreds of contaminants, and Bobbie holds it along with a Pesticide Free certification, reissued in March 2026. Kabrita, at the other end, publishes its own commissioned batch results, which is transparency worth crediting, but a brand paying for its own tests is the weakest tier of evidence we accept, and the independent lab that had no relationship with Kabrita put it in the worse choices tier.

One finding applies to everyone and deserves honest framing: CR found trace PFAS in nearly every formula tested, mostly obscure compounds at low levels, and one of its experts put the situation exactly right: parents cannot choose their way away from PFAS in an infant's diet. That is a supply chain problem, not a brand problem, and no pick in this article escapes it.

The ByHeart outbreak deserves the full story, because it is the most serious formula safety event since Sturgis and because we recommended the brand before it. In November 2025, ByHeart recalled all of its formula as the CDC and FDA investigated infant botulism cases; the outbreak was declared over in February 2026 at 48 infants in 17 states. The FDA's root cause work found a genetic match between C. botulinum in a formula sample and a sample of organic whole milk powder from a third party supplier, and no explanatory deficiencies at ByHeart's own plants. ByHeart returned to market in June 2026 under an FDA reviewed plan with a new C. botulinum testing protocol. All of that is to the company's credit, and none of it changes our position: after an outbreak that hospitalized 48 babies, we want to see a longer clean record before featuring the brand again. If you have ByHeart cans at home, note that recalled lots carry use by dates into December 2026, so check them against the recall list rather than the date.

The lawsuit file is dominated by NEC, necrotizing enterocolitis, an intestinal disease of premature infants. Hundreds of families have sued Abbott and Mead Johnson, arguing their cow's milk based preterm products, specialized NICU formulas and fortifiers rather than the cans on store shelves, raised NEC risk in preemies and that the companies failed to warn. Juries have split: 60 million dollars against Mead Johnson, 495 million against Abbott, then defense verdicts for both companies in later trials, and a federal docket of roughly 275 cases is still working through bellwethers. In September 2026 Abbott agreed to a 670 million dollar settlement of about 2,000 claims while denying liability. The science, for honesty's sake: the FDA, CDC and NIH issued a consensus statement in October 2024 concluding there is no conclusive evidence that preterm formula causes NEC, and strong evidence that human milk protects against it; prematurity itself is the dominant risk factor. We report the litigation as track record, not as proof of harm, and feeding decisions for premature infants belong to neonatologists, not to articles. But when a track record check asks which companies are spending 2026 in courtrooms over infant products, the answer is the two biggest names on the shelf.

And our pick is not spotless either, which we would rather tell you than have you find: the original Bobbie startup imported a German made formula that the FDA recalled in 2019 because its iron content was below the US minimum and its label did not comply; the recall was terminated in 2020, and the company relaunched in 2021 with the US made, FDA registered recipe sold today. That is the entire record, it predates the current product, and we count it as history rather than a strike. It is also, incidentally, a tidy illustration of why the US premarket rules exist, and why the gray market imports that skip them make us nervous.

Why Bobbie Is the Recommendation

Walk the four checks and the recommendation writes itself.

Ingredients. Organic lactose is the only carbohydrate, there is no corn syrup, glucose syrup or maltodextrin anywhere in the line, and no palm oil. The whey to casein ratio is set at 60 to 40 to match mature breast milk. The DHA is in at the EU required 20 mg per 100 kcal, extracted without hexane, and iron meets both the US and EU requirements at 1.2 mg per 100 kcal. It is the European style recipe, built inside the US regulatory system.

Packaging. A steel can, the format that carried the lowest microplastic counts in the powder packaging study, and Consumer Reports found no detectable BPA in any Bobbie formula.

Independent tests. This is where Bobbie separates. Top Choices tier in the 2025 round, and in the 2026 round all four Bobbie formulas tested non detect or low across lead, inorganic arsenic, cadmium, mercury, BPA and acrylamide. On top of the watchdog results sits Clean Label Project certification, both the Purity Award and the Pesticide Free mark, renewed in March 2026. No other brand family stacked both years of clean watchdog results and current independent certification.

Recalls and lawsuits. The current company's record is clean: no recalls of the US made formula, no litigation we could find after an adversarial search. The 2019 recall of the original imported product is disclosed above. Worth knowing on the durability of that record: since 2023 Bobbie has owned its own manufacturing, a plant in Heath, Ohio that was the first newly built formula facility in the US in four decades, which means its supply chain does not run through the contract manufacturers that appear repeatedly in the recall timeline.

The honest cons: Bobbie is a premium price product, sold on subscription from its own site and in cans at Target, Whole Foods, Walmart and other grocers. Its Amazon listings existed but had no buyable offer when we published, so our links go direct. It shares the whole category's trace PFAS reality. And it makes nothing for special medical needs: hypoallergenic and preterm nutrition stay with your pediatrician.

The Brands We Did Not Pick

2025 botulism outbreak, back on market since June 2026

ByHeart

The frustrating one, because the recipe and the testing record were genuinely good, and we said so in earlier coverage. Then came the November 2025 recall of every lot during the 48 infant botulism outbreak, traced by genetic match to a supplier's organic whole milk powder rather than to ByHeart's own plants. The company handled the aftermath seriously: full recall, a new C. botulinum testing protocol, an FDA reviewed return to market in June 2026. Our bar is unchanged by good crisis management: after an outbreak that hospitalized babies, a brand earns its way back with time, and ten weeks back on shelves is not a track record. Check any cans in your home against the recall list; recalled lots carry use by dates into December 2026.

Clean tests, then a toxin recall in Canada

Kendamil

The whole milk recipe that built the brand is real, its US sold formulas tested clean in Consumer Reports' rounds, and until February 2026 it was the obvious runner up. Then Canada recalled Kendamil whole milk formula sold at Costco for cereulide, a heat stable toxin produced by Bacillus cereus, in lots manufactured in May 2025. No US product was affected, and we state that plainly. But a bacterial toxin reaching finished cans anywhere in the supply chain is exactly what our fourth check exists to catch, and 2026 also saw Kendamil agree, after an advertising challenge, to discontinue several marketing claims including that its formula delivers better nutrition than competitors. Clean tests plus a fresh toxin recall lands at wait, not at pick.

Fine powder, wrong channel

HiPP and Holle

The European organic brands earn their reputation on ingredients, and the cans Consumer Reports got hold of tested clean. The problem is everything around the can: neither brand is FDA registered, US sales run through gray market importers, and in 2021 the FDA recalled a long list of imported HiPP and Holle products for iron below the US minimum and missing premarket notifications. An import outside the system also sits outside the recall notification chain, so if a batch is pulled in Europe, no one is obligated to tell an American parent. The European style recipe without the import gamble is exactly the niche Bobbie was built to fill.

The testing gap between marketing and watchdogs

Kabrita and Dr. Brown's SoothePro

Kabrita's goat milk formula markets heavily on gentleness and publishes its own commissioned batch testing, and Consumer Reports' independent round still placed it in the worse choices tier after detecting both arsenic and lead. Dr. Brown's SoothePro, a formula line made by Perrigo under the bottle brand's name, landed in the same tier. Neither result is a regulatory violation, and Kabrita disputes the framing. But when the brand's own numbers and the independent lab disagree, we go with the lab that was not paid by the brand.

Clean SKUs inside a heavy legal file

Similac and Enfamil

Evenhandedness requires both halves. Several mainline varieties, Similac 360 Total Care and Advance, Enfamil Gentlease and NeuroPro among them, tested clean in Consumer Reports' rounds, and millions of babies thrive on them. The record around them is why they are not picks: the highest arsenic readings in the 2025 round were both Abbott hypoallergenic formulas, Nutramigen was the only formula with detectable BPA, both companies spent 2022 to 2023 in the recall timeline, and both spent 2026 in NEC courtrooms, with Abbott settling roughly 2,000 claims for 670 million dollars in September. The federal science consensus says preterm formula is not conclusively a cause of NEC, and we report that alongside the settlement. A track record check is still a track record check.

The Prep Setup Sheds More Plastic Than the Powder

Here is the part of formula feeding this site exists to cover, and it is not the brand on the can. In 2020, researchers at Trinity College Dublin prepared formula in polypropylene baby bottles following standard WHO guidance and measured what came off the plastic: up to 16.2 million microplastic particles per liter. Heat drove the release: raising water temperature from 25 to 95 degrees Celsius pushed shedding from 0.6 million to 55 million particles per liter, and sterilizing the bottle made it worse. Their global estimate put the average bottle fed infant at about 1.6 million ingested particles a day, and over 4 million in the highest regions.

Where Formula Microplastics Actually Come From

Compare the numbers: roughly 49 particles a day from the powder, potentially millions from the bottle. Choosing the cleanest powder and preparing it in polypropylene is optimizing the wrong variable. The fix costs two swaps and no safety compromise:

  • Do the hot step in glass or steel. Formula prep for young infants uses water at 70 degrees Celsius or hotter to kill Cronobacter, which is precisely the temperature that maximizes plastic shedding. Boil, then mix powder and hot water in a glass container, let it cool to feeding temperature, and only then pour into the feeding bottle. Follow your pediatrician's prep guidance on water temperature; whatever it is, run the hot part of it in glass.
  • Feed from a glass or stainless bottle. Our baby bottle guide tested this category properly: Lifefactory glass is the best overall, Evenflo Classic Glass is the budget answer at a fraction of the price, and single wall stainless covers travel.
  • Mix with filtered water. Formula reconstitutes at roughly 87 percent water, so the water is most of the meal. Reverse osmosis is the standard we recommend for formula mixing: it removes lead, arsenic, PFAS and microplastics in one pass.
  • Measure with the scoop, stir with steel. The scoop is calibrated; keep it. The stirring spoon is not; make it stainless.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the safest baby formula in 2026?

Start with the reassuring baseline: the FDA's largest formula testing program ever, over 300 samples and 120,000 data points released in April 2026, found heavy metals below EPA drinking water limits across the board, and every formula on the regulated US shelf is nutritionally complete. Registration is a floor rather than a guarantee, though: independent testing keeps finding real differences between brands, and the recent recalls all happened inside the regulated system. Bobbie was the only brand family that cleared all four of our checks: lactose as the only carbohydrate with no palm oil, a steel can that tested non detect for BPA, Consumer Reports Top Choices placement in both the 2025 and 2026 rounds plus Clean Label Project certification, and a clean current recall and lawsuit record. Always talk to your pediatrician before switching a formula that is working.

Is European baby formula like HiPP or Holle safer than American formula?

The powder itself tested fine: Consumer Reports found low contaminant levels in both HiPP Dutch Stage 1 and Holle Bio Stage 1. The supply chain is the problem. Neither brand is FDA registered, so the versions sold in the US move through gray market importers, outside the US recall notification system. In 2021 the FDA recalled a long list of imported HiPP and Holle products because they contained less iron than the US minimum and had never filed the required premarket notification. If a European batch is ever recalled at home, no US system is obligated to tell you. An FDA registered formula built to the same European style recipe avoids the whole problem.

Is ByHeart formula safe now?

ByHeart recalled every lot of its formula in November 2025 during an infant botulism outbreak that sickened 48 babies across 17 states. The FDA later reported a genetic match between C. botulinum in a formula sample and a sample of organic whole milk powder from a third party supplier, and found no explanatory deficiencies at ByHeart's own plants. The outbreak was declared over in February 2026 and ByHeart returned to market in June 2026 under an FDA reviewed plan with a new C. botulinum testing protocol. Two cautions stand: recalled cans carry use by dates into December 2026, so check any can already in your home against the recall list, and we want to see a longer clean track record after an outbreak this serious before featuring the brand again.

Does baby formula contain microplastics?

Yes, but the powder is the small number. A 2023 peer reviewed study of 13 formula powders found an average of 42 microplastic particles per 100 grams, worst in composite box packaging and lowest in cans, which works out to roughly 49 particles a day for a formula fed infant. Preparation is the big number: the Nature Food bottle study found polypropylene baby bottles released up to 16.2 million particles per liter during standard formula prep, because hot water against plastic is exactly what drives release. The powder brand matters far less here than the bottle material. Prepare in glass or stainless steel and the exposure drops by orders of magnitude.

Which baby formulas did Consumer Reports flag?

In the March 2025 round of 41 powders, the highest inorganic arsenic readings were Abbott EleCare Hypoallergenic at 19.7 ppb and Similac Alimentum at 15.1 ppb, against the 10 ppb the EPA allows in drinking water. The other formulas on the worse choices list were Enfamil Nutramigen, the only formula where BPA and acrylamide were detected, Enfamil ProSobee Simply Plant Based, PurAmino Hypoallergenic, Similac NeoSure, Similac Total Comfort, Kabrita goat milk formula, Dr. Browns SoothePro, and Up and Up Soy. In the March 2026 round of 49 more formulas, 26 contained at least one contaminant at a level CR considers concerning, three powders exceeded its lead threshold, and every plant based formula tested was flagged for inorganic arsenic. Many formulas in both rounds also tested clean, including every Bobbie formula.

Is homemade or diluted baby formula ever safe?

No. This is the one place where the do it yourself instinct that serves you well everywhere else on this site can seriously hurt a baby. Homemade formula recipes have repeatedly caused hospitalizations for nutrient deficiencies, and diluting formula to stretch a can disrupts an infant's electrolyte balance. Nothing in this article is a reason to make either move. If cost is the constraint, several store brand formulas tested clean for heavy metals in Consumer Reports testing, WIC covers formula for qualifying families, and your pediatrician has seen this problem before and can help. A budget formula from a regulated US shelf, ideally one that tested clean, is a far safer way to feed a baby than homemade formula will ever be.
